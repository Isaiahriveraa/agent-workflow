# Dislikes and exceptions

Use this to capture what fails and why, not to build a blacklist of fashionable patterns. **No personal dislikes have been supplied or approved yet.**

## Two proposed anti-examples

These are hypothetical failure patterns, not accusations about the linked reference sites.

1. **The interface makes the student wait for a show.** Every section slides in, buttons bounce repeatedly, and reversing a drawer queues another animation. The problem is delayed work, distraction, and broken interruption—not the mere presence of animation. Try immediate feedback and a restrained, reversible transition. A rare expressive introduction may fit a different surface if it remains skippable and accessible.
2. **The copy cheers instead of helping.** “Unlock your limitless potential!” appears above a vague “Continue” button, while an incorrect answer gets “Oops, try harder!” The problem is missing meaning and next steps—not friendliness. Explain the task, label the actual action, and offer evidence-based feedback. Genuine specific encouragement is welcome.

## Record a real dislike

Use the same source, capture, evidence, and approval fields as `approved-examples.md`, plus the observed cost: confusion, waiting, discomfort, poor reading, or inappropriate tone. State where the pattern might still be appropriate. Do not convert a rejection on one student-facing screen into a universal ban.

## Defaults yield to context

Accessibility requirements, truthful content, and correct interaction behavior are not taste options. Visual heuristics are defaults: an existing design system, audience evidence, or approved brief can justify a different accent palette, radius scale, equal-weight grid, centered layout, or metadata separator.

Judge the effect on comprehension, hierarchy, wrapping, and access. A centered headline, dot separator, gradient, or repeated card is not by itself proof of generated or bad design. Preserve useful established patterns instead of redesigning them to satisfy a stylistic checklist.

If a reference conflicts with required accessibility or truthful product behavior, adapt or reject it and explain the concrete conflict. Approval of an aesthetic does not waive those requirements.

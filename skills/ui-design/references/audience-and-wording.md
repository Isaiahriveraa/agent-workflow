# Audience and wording

Read before drafting headings, explanations, labels, or feedback. Content is part of the interaction, not decoration added after the layout. Reuse the hub's `skills/impeccable/reference/clarify.md` for detailed UI-copy review; this reference adds audience and learning decisions rather than a second copy-editing system.

## Establish the reader and purpose

Use the existing product brief, research, terminology, and surrounding flow. Record only the missing decisions in the current task notes:

- **Reader:** their relevant stage, subject knowledge, language needs, and context. “Students” alone is not enough; a first-time learner and a graduate researcher need different explanations. Do not infer ability, age, or preferences from that label.
- **Purpose:** are they trying to do something, understand a concept, compare options, or decide whether to sign up?
- **Starting point:** what can they reasonably know already, and which term or step needs explaining?
- **Outcome:** what should they understand or be able to do after reading?
- **Evidence:** distinguish supplied facts and observed needs from assumptions. Ask one focused question when an unknown changes the explanation materially; otherwise state the assumption without presenting it as research.

## Structure by the job, not one universal template

| Surface | Lead with | Follow with |
|---|---|---|
| Task or submission | What to do | Requirements, ordered steps, actual consequence or confirmation |
| Lesson or explanation | The idea and why it matters to this task | Define unfamiliar terms, show a relevant example, then offer practice or a check |
| Dashboard or progress | What the status means | Relevant comparison and next useful action; distinguish completion from demonstrated understanding |
| Choice or pricing | What the options change | Relevant differences, costs, limits, and consequences before commitment |
| Error or blocked action | What did not happen | Known cause if useful, preserved work, and a recovery route |
| Marketing | Concrete value for the reader | Supported evidence and an honest next step, not promises of guaranteed outcomes |

Use descriptive headings, short meaningful chunks, and instructions beside the action. Put essential requirements before commitment, not inside a tooltip. Use lists for steps, tables for comparisons, and prose for reasoning. Progressive disclosure may hide uncommon detail, not information needed to make a safe decision.

## Explain without talking down

- Use the audience's familiar words. Retain necessary subject terms and define them on first use; plain language should not erase what students need to learn.
- For a new concept, start with a clear explanation, a relevant example, and a next step. Add counterexamples or deeper reasoning when they resolve a likely misconception. Shortest is not always clearest.
- Write like a thoughtful teacher or colleague. Avoid forced slang, exaggerated enthusiasm, patronizing reassurance, and generic promises such as “unlock your potential.” Warmth comes from useful help and honest encouragement, not a catchphrase ban.
- Give feedback about the work: what is correct, what needs attention, and one usable next step. Praise should identify something observable. Do not claim mastery from a click, shame mistakes, or assign personality labels.
- Do not invent deadlines, grades, study times, progress, teacher approval, product capabilities, or learning benefits to make copy feel specific. Mark unknown facts for confirmation.
- Keep one term for each concept. Make buttons describe the actual action; “Save draft” and “Submit assignment” are different commitments.

## Examples, not product facts

**Generic:** “Embark on your learning journey and master persuasive writing!”

**Useful:** “Practice writing a persuasive paragraph. State your opinion, support it with evidence, and explain how the evidence supports your point.”

**Empty feedback:** “Not quite! Try harder.”

**Useful, only if supported by the work:** “You stated your opinion. Now add one example that supports it.”

**Unclear action:** “Continue.” **Specific action:** “Review answers,” when that is what happens next. Do not relabel a submission as a harmless review.

## Verify understanding in context

Read the whole path, including empty, loading, success, and failure states. Check factual accuracy, terminology, realistic narrow layouts, accessible labels, and whether each sentence adds information. Ask representative readers to explain the message in their own words and show what they would do next, without coaching. For learning content, check whether they can apply the explanation, not merely repeat it.

A readability score, designer opinion, or model review is a screening aid, not proof that students understand. Report who was tested, what they understood, and what remains assumed. Do not declare the copy “student-friendly” without evidence.

## Sources and limits

- [W3C: Use Clear Words](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG2/supplemental/patterns/o3p01-clear-words/): familiar language and explained terminology.
- [W3C: Use Clear Step-by-step Instructions](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG2/supplemental/patterns/o4p07-step-instructions/): guidance near the task, complete steps, and examples. Both W3C pages are supplemental guidance, not additional WCAG conformance requirements.
- [Education Endowment Foundation: Feedback](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/feedback): clear, actionable feedback and opportunities to use it. School-learning evidence informs the learning guidance; it does not prove a particular website's copy improves attainment.

Sources consulted 2026-10-15. The surface table and examples are local recommendations, not quotations or universal prescriptions.
